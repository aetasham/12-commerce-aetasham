1. National Income

National income measures the value of final goods and services generated by an economy during a period. GDP measures production within domestic territory; GNP adjusts GDP for net factor income from abroad.

Methods

  • Value Added Method
  • Income Method
  • Expenditure Method
GDP = value of final production within domestic territory. Real GDP uses constant prices; Nominal GDP uses current prices.
Q1. What is GDP?
A. The monetary value of final goods and services produced within a country's domestic territory during a specified period.
Q2. Why are intermediate goods excluded?
A. To avoid double counting because their value is already included in final goods.
Q3. Name the three methods of national income estimation.
A. Value added/product method, income method and expenditure method.

2. Money

Money is anything generally accepted as a means of payment. It solves the double coincidence of wants problem of barter.

Functions

  • Medium of exchange
  • Measure of value
  • Store of value
  • Standard of deferred payments
Q1. What is money?
A. Anything generally accepted as a means of payment for goods, services and debts.
Q2. How does money solve barter's difficulty?
A. It acts as a common medium of exchange, removing the need for double coincidence of wants.

3. Banking & Central Bank

Commercial banks accept deposits and provide loans. A central bank regulates the banking system and uses monetary policy to influence credit and liquidity.

Functions

  • Commercial bank: deposits, loans, payments and credit creation.
  • Central bank: currency issue, banker to government, banker to banks, credit control and reserves.
Q1. What is a commercial bank?
A. A financial institution that accepts deposits from the public and provides loans and other banking services.
Q2. What is monetary policy?
A. Policy through which the monetary authority influences money supply, credit and interest conditions to achieve economic objectives.

4. Determination of Income and Employment

Aggregate demand represents planned expenditure. In the simple model, equilibrium output occurs when planned aggregate demand equals output.

Multiplier k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS
Q1. What is MPC?
A. Marginal Propensity to Consume: the change in consumption caused by a change in income.
Q2. If MPC = 0.75, find multiplier.
A. k = 1/(1−0.75) = 4.
Q3. If autonomous expenditure rises by ₹100 crore and k=4, what is change in equilibrium income?
A. ΔY = k × ΔA = 4 × ₹100 crore = ₹400 crore.

5. Government Budget

A government budget is an annual statement of estimated receipts and expenditure.

Components

Receipts: revenue and capital. Expenditure: revenue and capital.

Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding borrowings
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments
Q1. Revenue receipts?
A. Receipts that do not create liabilities or reduce government assets, such as tax and many non-tax receipts.
Q2. What is fiscal deficit?
A. The excess of total expenditure over total receipts excluding borrowings.

6. Balance of Payments

Balance of Payments records economic transactions between residents of an economy and the rest of the world.

Current account includes goods, services, income and transfers. Capital/financial transactions cover flows affecting external assets and liabilities according to classification.

Q1. What is exchange rate?
A. The price of one currency expressed in terms of another currency.
Q2. What is current account?
A. It records transactions in goods, services, income and current transfers.

7. Indian Economy on the Eve of Independence

The colonial economy had low industrial development, heavy dependence on agriculture, limited infrastructure for broad development and poor living conditions.

Q1. Describe agriculture under colonial rule.
A. It suffered from low productivity, inadequate investment and exploitative land arrangements, with limited diversification and technology.
Q2. What was the industrial condition?
A. Modern industries were limited and traditional handicrafts faced severe decline and competition.

8. Indian Economy 1950–1990

India adopted planned development, public sector expansion, industrialisation, self-reliance and agricultural development. The Green Revolution increased food-grain production in selected regions.

Q1. What is planning?
A. Deliberate setting of development objectives and allocation of resources to achieve them over a period.
Q2. What was the Green Revolution?
A. A major increase in agricultural productivity using high-yielding varieties, irrigation, fertilisers and improved practices, especially in selected areas.

9. Economic Reforms Since 1991

Reforms included liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation. They aimed to reduce unnecessary controls, increase competition and integrate the economy with the world economy.

Q1. What is liberalisation?
A. Reduction of unnecessary government controls and restrictions on economic activity.
Q2. What is privatisation?
A. Greater role for private ownership/management and reduction of the relative role of the public sector in suitable activities.
Q3. What is globalisation?
A. Increasing integration of the domestic economy with the world economy through trade, investment, technology and other linkages.

10. Human Capital Formation

Human capital is the stock of knowledge, skills and health embodied in people. Education and healthcare improve productivity and development.

Q1. Why is education an investment?
A. It improves knowledge and skills, raising productivity, employability and potential earnings.
Q2. What is human capital formation?
A. The process of increasing people's productive capabilities through education, health, training and related investments.

11. Rural Development

Rural development includes measures for better credit, marketing, infrastructure, diversification, productivity and employment.

Q1. Why is rural credit important?
A. Timely affordable credit supports productive investment and reduces dependence on exploitative informal borrowing.
Q2. Why diversify rural livelihoods?
A. It reduces dependence on agriculture and creates additional income and employment opportunities.

12. Employment

Employment concepts include worker, labour force, unemployment and different employment structures. Policies aim to create productive and sustainable work opportunities.

Q1. What is unemployment?
A. A situation where people willing and able to work at prevailing conditions do not get suitable work.
Q2. Why is quality employment important?
A. It supports income, productivity, dignity and inclusive economic development.

13. Poverty

Poverty is a condition in which people lack sufficient resources to meet basic needs. Causes include low income, unemployment, unequal opportunities and inadequate access to education and health.

Q1. What is poverty?
A. Inability to attain minimum acceptable levels of living and basic capabilities according to the relevant poverty measure.
Q2. How can poverty be reduced?
A. Through productive employment, education, healthcare, infrastructure, targeted support and inclusive growth policies.

14. Environment & Sustainable Development

Sustainable development seeks to meet present needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet their needs. It requires efficient resource use, pollution control and balanced growth.

Q1. What is sustainable development?
A. Development that meets present needs while preserving the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
Q2. How can sustainability be promoted?
A. Through cleaner technology, efficient resource use, renewable energy, pollution control, conservation and responsible consumption.
