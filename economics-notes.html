📈 PART A — MACRO ECONOMICS

Macro Economics deals with economy-wide variables such as national income, aggregate demand, money, banking, government budget and external sector.

1. National Income and Related Aggregates

National income accounting measures the value of final goods and services and income generated in an economy during a period.

Methods

  • Value Added Method
  • Income Method
  • Expenditure Method
GDP measures production within domestic territory. Real GDP uses constant prices; Nominal GDP uses current prices.
Q1. What is GDP?
A. Monetary value of final goods and services produced within domestic territory during a specified period.
Q2. Why are intermediate goods excluded?
A. To avoid double counting because their value is included in final goods.
Q3. Name three methods.
A. Value added/product, income and expenditure methods.

2. Money and Banking

Money is generally accepted as a means of payment and solves the double coincidence of wants problem.

  • Medium of exchange
  • Measure of value
  • Store of value
  • Standard of deferred payments

Commercial banks accept deposits and provide loans. The central bank regulates banking and monetary conditions.

Q1. What is money?
A. Anything generally accepted as a means of payment.
Q2. What is monetary policy?
A. Policy through which the monetary authority influences money, credit and interest conditions to achieve economic objectives.

3. Determination of Income and Employment

Aggregate demand represents planned expenditure. In the simple model, equilibrium occurs when planned aggregate demand equals output.

Multiplier k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS
Q1. What is MPC?
A. Marginal Propensity to Consume: change in consumption divided by change in income.
Q2. If MPC = 0.75, find multiplier.
A. k = 1/(1−0.75) = 4.
Q3. If autonomous expenditure rises ₹100 crore and k=4?
A. Change in equilibrium income = 4 × ₹100 crore = ₹400 crore.

4. Government Budget and the Economy

A government budget is an annual statement of estimated receipts and expenditure.

Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding borrowings
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments
Q1. What are revenue receipts?
A. Receipts that generally do not create liabilities or reduce government assets, such as tax and many non-tax receipts.
Q2. What is fiscal deficit?
A. Excess of total expenditure over total receipts excluding borrowings.

5. Balance of Payments

Balance of Payments records economic transactions between residents of an economy and the rest of the world.

Current account covers goods, services, income and current transfers. Capital/financial transactions cover external assets and liabilities according to classification.

Q1. What is exchange rate?
A. Price of one currency expressed in terms of another.
Q2. What is current account?
A. It records transactions in goods, services, income and current transfers.

🇮🇳 PART B — INDIAN ECONOMY

Indian Economy focuses on India's development experience, reforms, human capital, rural development, employment, poverty and sustainable development.

1. Indian Economy on the Eve of Independence

The colonial economy had low industrial development, heavy dependence on agriculture, limited infrastructure for broad development and poor living conditions.

Q1. Describe agriculture under colonial rule.
A. It suffered from low productivity, inadequate investment and exploitative arrangements, with limited diversification and technology.
Q2. What was the industrial condition?
A. Modern industries were limited while traditional handicrafts faced decline and competition.

2. Indian Economy 1950–1990

India adopted planned development with emphasis on public sector, industrialisation, self-reliance and agricultural development.

Q1. What is economic planning?
A. Deliberate setting of development objectives and allocation of resources to achieve them over a period.
Q2. What was the Green Revolution?
A. A major increase in agricultural productivity using high-yielding varieties, irrigation, fertilisers and improved practices, especially in selected regions.

3. Economic Reforms Since 1991

Reforms included liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation (LPG), aimed at reducing unnecessary controls, increasing competition and integrating with the world economy.

Q1. What is liberalisation?
A. Reduction of unnecessary government controls and restrictions on economic activity.
Q2. What is privatisation?
A. Increasing the role of private ownership/management in suitable economic activities.
Q3. What is globalisation?
A. Increasing integration of the domestic economy with the world economy through trade, investment, technology and other linkages.

4. Human Capital Formation

Human capital is the stock of knowledge, skills and health embodied in people. Education, training and healthcare improve productive capabilities.

Q1. Why is education an investment?
A. It improves skills and productivity and can increase employability and earnings.
Q2. What is human capital formation?
A. The process of increasing people's productive capabilities through education, health, training and related investments.

5. Rural Development

Rural development includes better credit, marketing, infrastructure, diversification, productivity and employment.

Q1. Why is rural credit important?
A. Timely affordable credit supports productive investment and reduces dependence on exploitative informal borrowing.
Q2. Why diversify rural livelihoods?
A. It reduces dependence on agriculture and creates additional income and employment opportunities.

6. Employment

Employment concepts include worker, labour force, unemployment and different employment structures.

Q1. What is unemployment?
A. A situation where people willing and able to work do not obtain suitable work.
Q2. Why is quality employment important?
A. It supports income, productivity, dignity and inclusive development.

7. Poverty

Poverty is a condition in which people lack sufficient resources to meet basic needs. Causes include low income, unemployment and unequal opportunities.

Q1. What is poverty?
A. Inability to attain minimum acceptable levels of living and basic capabilities according to the relevant poverty measure.
Q2. How can poverty be reduced?
A. Through productive employment, education, healthcare, infrastructure, targeted support and inclusive growth policies.

8. Environment & Sustainable Development

Sustainable development meets present needs while preserving the ability of future generations to meet their needs.

Q1. What is sustainable development?
A. Development that meets present needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet their needs.
Q2. How can sustainability be promoted?
A. Through cleaner technology, efficient resource use, renewable energy, pollution control, conservation and responsible consumption.
