📘 ACCOUNTANCY

Complete revision coverage: concepts, formulas, adjustments and exam points.

Accountancy 1 — Partnership Fundamentals

Partnership is the relationship between persons who agree to share profits of a business carried on by all or any one acting for all. Key ideas: agreement, business, profit sharing, mutual agency and competent partners.

Partnership deed: records capital, ratio, drawings, interest, salary/commission and other terms.

  • If deed is silent, profits/losses are generally shared equally.
  • No interest on capital or drawings unless agreed.
  • Partner salary/commission requires an agreement.
  • Capital may be fixed or fluctuating.
  • Profit and Loss Appropriation Account distributes profit after agreed appropriations.
Partner's Share = Distributable Profit × Partner's Profit-Sharing Fraction

Accountancy 2 — Goodwill

Goodwill represents the reputation and other advantages that help a firm earn above-normal profits.

Average Profit = Total Adjusted Profits ÷ Number of Years
Goodwill = Average Profit × Years' Purchase
Normal Profit = Capital Employed × Normal Rate ÷ 100
Super Profit = Average Profit − Normal Profit
Goodwill = Super Profit × Years' Purchase
Capitalised Value = Average Profit × 100 ÷ Normal Rate
Goodwill = Capitalised Value − Actual Capital Employed

Adjust abnormal gains/losses and expected future changes before calculating adjusted average profit.

Accountancy 3 — Change in Profit-Sharing Ratio

Existing partners can revise their ratio by agreement.

Sacrificing Ratio = Old Share − New Share
Gaining Ratio = New Share − Old Share

Adjust goodwill, reserves, accumulated profits/losses and revaluation according to the question.

Accountancy 4 — Admission of a Partner

A new partner is admitted with consent of existing partners and receives a share of future profits.

  1. Calculate new ratio.
  2. Calculate sacrificing ratio.
  3. Adjust goodwill.
  4. Revalue assets and liabilities.
  5. Adjust reserves and accumulated profits/losses.
  6. Bring in capital and other agreed amounts.
  7. Prepare revised Balance Sheet.

Increase in asset/decrease in liability creates revaluation gain; decrease in asset/increase in liability creates revaluation loss.

Accountancy 5 — Retirement of a Partner

On retirement, continuing partners carry on the business.

Gaining Ratio = New Share − Old Share
  • Calculate new ratio and gaining ratio.
  • Adjust goodwill in favour of retiring partner.
  • Record revaluation profit/loss.
  • Distribute reserves and accumulated items.
  • Calculate amount due and settlement.

Accountancy 6 — Death of a Partner

The deceased partner's estate receives the amount due up to the date of death.

  • Capital balance
  • Goodwill share
  • Reserves/accumulated profits
  • Revaluation profit/loss
  • Profit up to date of death
  • Less drawings and applicable deductions

Profit up to death may be estimated using time or turnover basis when the question supplies the required information.

Accountancy 7 — Dissolution

Dissolution closes the firm and settles assets and liabilities. Realisation Account records disposal of assets and settlement of liabilities.

  1. Realise assets and settle external liabilities.
  2. Settle partner loan according to applicable order.
  3. Settle partners' capitals.
  4. Distribute remaining cash.

Realisation profit/loss is transferred to partners in their profit-sharing ratio.

Accountancy 8 — Share Capital

Share capital is raised by a company through issue of shares. Terms: authorised, issued, subscribed, called-up and paid-up capital.

  • Application and allotment
  • Calls
  • Oversubscription and pro-rata allotment
  • Calls in arrears/advance
  • Forfeiture
  • Reissue

Forfeiture cancels shares for non-payment according to issue terms; reissue is subject to applicable company law and accounting treatment.

Accountancy 9 — Debentures

Debentures are debt instruments and holders are creditors.

  • Issue at par/premium or discount where permitted.
  • Issue for consideration other than cash.
  • Interest on debentures.
  • Redemption according to terms.
Interest = Nominal Value × Rate × Time ÷ 100

Accountancy 10 — Financial Statements

Balance Sheet shows financial position at a particular date. Statement of Profit and Loss reports income, expenses and profit/loss for a period. Notes to Accounts provide supporting details and disclosures.

Learn prescribed headings and classify every item carefully before preparing the statement.

Accountancy 11 — Analysis of Financial Statements

Analysis evaluates liquidity, solvency, profitability and efficiency.

Absolute Change = Current Year − Previous Year
Percentage Change = Absolute Change ÷ Previous Year × 100

Comparative statements compare years; common-size statements express items as percentages of a common base; ratio analysis studies relationships between figures.

Accountancy 12 — Accounting Ratios

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio = Quick Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Debt-Equity Ratio = Long-term Debt ÷ Shareholders' Funds
Proprietary Ratio = Shareholders' Funds ÷ Total Assets
Inventory Turnover = Cost of Revenue from Operations ÷ Average Inventory
Trade Receivables Turnover = Net Credit Revenue from Operations ÷ Average Trade Receivables
Working Capital Turnover = Revenue from Operations ÷ Working Capital
Gross Profit Ratio = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100
Net Profit Ratio = Net Profit ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100
Operating Ratio = Operating Cost ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100

Accountancy 13 — Cash Flow Statement

Cash Flow Statement explains changes in cash and cash equivalents.

  • Operating: main revenue-generating activities.
  • Investing: purchase/sale of long-term assets and investments.
  • Financing: changes in equity and borrowings.

Under indirect method, start with accounting profit and adjust for non-cash/non-operating items and working-capital changes.

Net Change in Cash = Operating + Investing + Financing Cash Flow

Business Studies 1 — Nature and Significance of Management

Management is planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling resources to achieve goals effectively and efficiently.

  • Objectives: organisational, social and personal.
  • Importance: goal achievement, efficiency, adaptation, employee development and social contribution.
  • Levels: top, middle and supervisory.
  • Management has science, art and profession characteristics.

Coordination integrates individual and departmental efforts and is continuous and pervasive.

Business Studies 2 — Principles of Management

Principles are broad flexible guidelines.

Fayol: division of work, authority and responsibility, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, subordination of individual interest, remuneration, centralisation/decentralisation, scalar chain, order, equity, stability, initiative and esprit de corps.

Taylor: science not rule of thumb, harmony not discord, cooperation not individualism, development of each person. Techniques include functional foremanship, standardisation, method/time/motion/fatigue studies and differential piece rate.

Business Studies 3 — Business Environment

External forces affecting business. Features: totality, specific/general forces, interrelatedness, dynamic nature, uncertainty and relativity. Dimensions: economic, social, technological, political and legal. Importance: opportunities, threats, first-mover advantage, planning and adaptation.

Business Studies 4 — Planning

Planning decides in advance what, how, when and who.

  • Features: goal-oriented, primary, pervasive, continuous, futuristic and decision-making.
  • Importance: direction, uncertainty reduction, reduced overlap, innovation, decisions and control standards.
  • Limitations: rigidity, reduced creativity, time/cost, changing environment and no guarantee.
  • Plans: objectives, strategy, policy, procedure, method, rule, programme and budget.

Business Studies 5 — Organising

Steps: divide work, departmentalise, assign duties and establish reporting relationships. Structures include functional and divisional. Formal organisation is planned; informal develops socially.

Delegation: authority + responsibility + accountability. Decentralisation: systematic dispersal of decision-making authority.

Business Studies 6 — Staffing

Process: manpower planning, recruitment, selection, placement/orientation, training/development, appraisal, promotion/career planning and compensation. Recruitment attracts candidates; selection chooses suitable candidates. Training improves present-job skills; development prepares for broader responsibilities.

Business Studies 7 — Directing

Elements: supervision, motivation, leadership and communication.

Maslow: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation. Leadership styles: autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire. Communication can be formal/informal and upward/downward/horizontal. Barriers may be semantic, psychological, organisational or personal.

Business Studies 8 — Controlling

  1. Set standards.
  2. Measure actual performance.
  3. Compare with standards.
  4. Analyse deviations.
  5. Take corrective action.

Planning sets standards and controlling provides feedback. Critical-point control and management by exception focus attention on important deviations.

Business Studies 9 — Financial Management

Three major decisions: investment, financing and dividend.

  • Investment considers return, risk and long-term asset decisions.
  • Financing decides debt/equity mix.
  • Dividend decides distribution versus retention.

Capital structure is the debt-equity mix. Working capital supports daily operations.

Business Studies 10 — Financial Markets

Money market deals mainly in short-term funds; capital market in long-term funds. Primary market handles new securities; secondary market trades existing securities. SEBI regulates the securities market and protects investors.

Business Studies 11 — Marketing Management

Marketing identifies needs and creates, communicates and delivers value.

4 Ps: Product, Price, Place and Promotion. Product includes quality, design, branding, packaging and labelling. Price depends on cost, demand, competition and objectives. Place covers channels and distribution. Promotion includes advertising, personal selling, sales promotion and public relations.

Business Studies 12 — Consumer Protection

Rights: safety, information, choice, being heard, redressal and consumer education. Responsibilities: quality consciousness, reading labels, insisting on bills, proper use and seeking genuine redressal. Use the applicable statutory consumer-protection mechanism.

📊 ECONOMICS

Macroeconomics plus Indian Economic Development: definitions, diagrams, formulas, causes, effects and policy points.

Economics 1 — National Income and Related Aggregates

GDP is the value of final goods and services produced within domestic territory during a period. GNP adjusts GDP for net factor income from abroad. Distinguish gross/net, domestic/national and market price/factor cost concepts as required.

Methods: value added/product method, income method and expenditure method.

National Income = NNP at Factor Cost (in the standard textbook framework)
Real GDP uses constant prices; Nominal GDP uses current prices.

Avoid double counting by counting final goods or adding value added.

Economics 2 — Money and Banking

Money functions as medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value and standard of deferred payments.

Commercial banks accept deposits and create credit subject to reserve and regulatory conditions. The central bank regulates the monetary and banking system and uses monetary policy tools such as policy rates, reserve requirements and open market operations.

Economics 3 — Determination of Income and Employment

Aggregate demand is planned expenditure. Equilibrium occurs when planned aggregate demand equals output.

Multiplier k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) in the simple model
MPC = Change in Consumption ÷ Change in Income

Autonomous spending changes can create a multiplied change in equilibrium income.

Economics 4 — Government Budget and the Economy

Government budget is an annual statement of estimated receipts and expenditure.

  • Revenue receipts and capital receipts.
  • Revenue expenditure and capital expenditure.
  • Revenue deficit.
  • Fiscal deficit.
  • Primary deficit.
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding borrowings

Budget objectives include allocation, redistribution, economic stability and growth.

Economics 5 — Balance of Payments

Balance of Payments records economic transactions between residents and the rest of the world.

  • Current account: goods, services, income and transfers.
  • Capital/financial account: transactions affecting external assets and liabilities, as classified in the syllabus.

Exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. Appreciation and depreciation affect imports and exports.

Economics 6 — Indian Economy on the Eve of Independence

The colonial economy had low industrial development, heavy dependence on agriculture, limited productive infrastructure and poor living conditions. Industrial structure was weak and external trade patterns reflected colonial interests.

Economics 7 — Indian Economy 1950–1990

Development planning emphasised public sector, industrialisation, self-reliance and agricultural development. The Green Revolution increased food-grain output in selected regions and periods.

Common planning goals: growth, modernisation, self-reliance and equity.

Economics 8 — Economic Reforms Since 1991

Reforms are commonly grouped as liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation. They reduced many controls, increased competition and integrated India more closely with the world economy.

  • Liberalisation: reducing unnecessary restrictions.
  • Privatisation: greater role for private sector and changes in public-sector participation.
  • Globalisation: stronger integration with world markets.

Economics 9 — Human Capital Formation

Human capital is the stock of knowledge, skills and health embodied in people. Education and health improve productivity and can support economic development.

Sources include investment by individuals, families, firms and government in education, health, training and information.

Economics 10 — Rural Development

Rural development covers better credit, marketing, infrastructure, diversification of livelihoods and productivity.

  • Institutional rural credit helps reduce dependence on informal sources.
  • Agricultural marketing needs storage, transport, grading and organised markets.
  • Diversification can include allied activities and non-farm employment.

Economics 11 — Employment

Important concepts include worker, labour force, workforce, unemployment and employment structure.

Types of unemployment discussed in economics include open, seasonal, disguised and educated unemployment. Policies aim to create productive and sustainable employment.

Economics 12 — Poverty

Poverty is inadequate access to resources needed for a basic standard of living. Causes can include low income, unemployment, unequal opportunities and limited access to education and health.

Anti-poverty strategies combine growth, employment, social protection, human-capital development and targeted programmes.

Economics 13 — Environment and Sustainable Development

Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations. It requires efficient resource use, pollution control and balanced economic growth.

  • Renewable vs non-renewable resources.
  • Pollution control and waste management.
  • Conservation and efficient production/consumption.
  • Environmental awareness and appropriate policy.

Economics 14 — Development Experience and Comparison

Economic development can be compared using income, human development, health, education, employment and structural indicators. Comparisons should consider both quantitative and qualitative measures rather than relying on one number.

For comparison questions, write the indicator, explain what it measures, then compare the economies using the data or facts supplied.

💻 INFORMATICS PRACTICES

Python/Pandas, data visualisation, SQL, databases, networks, web and cyber safety.

IP 1 — Pandas Series

A Series is a one-dimensional labelled data structure. It stores values with an associated index.

  • Create Series from list, dictionary or scalar with appropriate syntax.
  • Access elements using labels/positions as applicable.
  • Common properties include index and values.

IP 2 — Pandas DataFrame

A DataFrame is a two-dimensional labelled tabular data structure with rows and columns.

  • Create from dictionaries/lists or other supported data.
  • Select columns and rows.
  • Add, rename and delete columns.
  • Use head(), tail(), shape, columns, index and describe() for inspection where applicable.

IP 3 — Data Handling and Analysis

Data handling includes cleaning, organising, filtering, sorting and transforming data.

  • Filtering selects rows meeting a condition.
  • Sorting arranges values according to selected columns.
  • Missing values should be identified and handled appropriately.
  • Aggregation and grouping help summarise data.

IP 4 — Data Visualisation

Visualisation presents data graphically.

  • Line chart: trends.
  • Bar chart: category comparison.
  • Histogram: distribution of numerical data.
  • Labels, title and legend should make a chart understandable.

Matplotlib is commonly used for Python plotting.

IP 5 — SQL

SQL means Structured Query Language and is used with relational databases.

  • SELECT retrieves data.
  • WHERE filters records.
  • ORDER BY sorts results.
  • GROUP BY groups rows.
  • HAVING filters groups where applicable.
  • COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN and MAX are aggregate functions.
  • Operators include arithmetic, relational and logical operators.
Typical form: SELECT column_list FROM table WHERE condition ORDER BY column;

IP 6 — Database Concepts

Database is an organised collection of data. DBMS means Database Management System; RDBMS means Relational Database Management System.

A table contains rows/records and columns/fields. A primary key uniquely identifies records. A foreign key can reference a key in another table to establish relationships.

IP 7 — Computer Networks

  • LAN: Local Area Network.
  • MAN: Metropolitan Area Network.
  • WAN: Wide Area Network.
  • Switch connects devices within a network.
  • Router forwards packets between networks.
  • Access point provides wireless network access.

Network topologies describe how devices are connected. Common ideas include bus, star, ring and mesh.

IP 8 — Internet and Web

URL = Uniform Resource Locator. HTTP = Hypertext Transfer Protocol. HTTPS = Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure. HTML = HyperText Markup Language.

Web browsers request and display web resources. HTTPS adds encryption and authentication through TLS.

IP 9 — Cyber Safety and Ethics

  • Use strong unique passwords and protect login credentials.
  • Use multi-factor authentication where available.
  • Be cautious with suspicious links, attachments and messages.
  • Keep software updated and maintain backups.
  • Respect privacy, copyright, intellectual property and digital rights.
  • Report genuine cyber incidents through appropriate channels.

Ethics: responsible digital behaviour includes honesty, privacy, respect, lawful use and avoiding unauthorised access or misuse.
