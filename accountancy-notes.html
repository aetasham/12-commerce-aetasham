1. Partnership Fundamentals

Complete Concept

Partnership is the relationship between persons who agree to share profits of a business carried on by all or any one of them acting for all. The essential elements are agreement, business, profit sharing, mutual agency and competent partners.

Partnership Deed

The deed records capital, profit-sharing ratio, drawings, interest on capital/drawings, salary or commission, admission, retirement, goodwill and other terms.

If deed is silent

  • Profits and losses are shared equally.
  • No interest on capital is allowed.
  • No interest on drawings is charged.
  • No partner gets salary/commission merely for working.
  • Interest on partner's loan is treated according to applicable partnership rules.

Capital Methods

Fixed capital keeps the Capital Account generally unchanged and uses a Current Account for regular adjustments. Fluctuating capital records drawings, interest, salary and profit directly in the Capital Account.

Important Q&A

Q1. What is partnership?
A. It is a contractual relationship between persons who agree to carry on a business and share its profits, with mutual agency.
Q2. What is a partnership deed?
A. It is the written agreement containing the terms and conditions governing the partnership.
Q3. What is mutual agency?
A. Every partner is both an agent of the firm and the other partners and a principal for himself/herself.
Q4. Distinguish fixed and fluctuating capital.
A. Under fixed capital, regular adjustments are recorded in Current Accounts; under fluctuating capital, they are recorded in Capital Accounts.

Exam practice: Prepare Capital and Current Accounts from a partnership deed and identify the treatment of each item.

2. Goodwill

Goodwill represents the value of reputation and advantages that enable a firm to earn above-normal profits.

Average Profit = Total Adjusted Profits ÷ Number of Years
Goodwill = Average Profit × Years' Purchase
Normal Profit = Capital Employed × Normal Rate ÷ 100
Super Profit = Average Profit − Normal Profit
Goodwill = Super Profit × Years' Purchase
Capitalised Value = Average Profit × 100 ÷ Normal Rate
Goodwill = Capitalised Value − Actual Capital Employed

Important Q&A

Q1. Why is goodwill valued?
A. It is valued when there is a change in the partnership relationship, such as admission, retirement, death or change in ratio.
Q2. What is super profit?
A. Super profit is the excess of average profit over normal profit.
Q3. A firm earns average profit ₹60,000, normal rate is 10% and capital employed ₹5,00,000. Find super profit.
A. Normal profit = ₹5,00,000 × 10% = ₹50,000. Super profit = ₹60,000 − ₹50,000 = ₹10,000.
Q4. Average profit is ₹80,000 and years' purchase is 3. Find goodwill.
A. Goodwill = ₹80,000 × 3 = ₹2,40,000.

3. Change in Profit-Sharing Ratio

Existing partners may change their ratio by mutual agreement. Adjustments normally include goodwill, reserves and revaluation.

Sacrificing Ratio = Old Share − New Share
Gaining Ratio = New Share − Old Share
Q1. What is sacrificing ratio?
A. It is the proportion in which old partners sacrifice their profit share in favour of another partner.
Q2. What is gaining ratio?
A. It is the proportion in which continuing partners gain the share of a retiring partner.
Q3. Old ratio of A and B is 3:2 and new ratio is 1:1. Find sacrifice/gain.
A. A: old 3/5, new 1/2, so A sacrifices 1/10. B: old 2/5, new 1/2, so B gains 1/10.

4. Admission of a Partner

A new partner is admitted with the consent of existing partners. The incoming partner generally brings capital and goodwill and receives a future profit share.

Complete Procedure

  1. Calculate new ratio.
  2. Calculate sacrificing ratio.
  3. Adjust goodwill.
  4. Revalue assets and liabilities.
  5. Distribute reserves and accumulated profits/losses.
  6. Record new partner's capital.
  7. Prepare revised Balance Sheet.
Q1. Why is sacrificing ratio calculated?
A. To determine the proportion in which old partners sacrifice their shares for the incoming partner.
Q2. What happens to revaluation profit on admission?
A. It is transferred to old partners' Capital Accounts in their old profit-sharing ratio.
Q3. A new partner brings ₹40,000 capital and ₹20,000 goodwill premium. What is the accounting idea?
A. Capital is credited to the incoming partner's Capital Account; goodwill premium is credited to sacrificing partners in their sacrificing ratio when brought in cash, subject to the question.

5. Retirement of a Partner

On retirement, continuing partners carry on the business and the retiring partner's amount is settled.

Gaining Ratio = New Share − Old Share
Q1. Why is gaining ratio calculated?
A. Continuing partners compensate the retiring partner for the share of goodwill they gain.
Q2. What adjustments are made at retirement?
A. Goodwill, revaluation, reserves/accumulated profits or losses and the amount due to the retiring partner are adjusted.
Q3. If retiring partner's amount is not paid immediately, what can be done?
A. The amount due may be transferred to the retiring partner's Loan Account as required by the question.

6. Death of a Partner

The deceased partner's estate receives the amount due up to the date of death.

Amount may include

  • Capital balance
  • Goodwill share
  • Reserve/profit share
  • Revaluation profit share
  • Profit up to date of death
  • Less drawings, losses and other deductions
Q1. How is profit up to death calculated?
A. It may be estimated on time basis or sales/turnover basis when the necessary information is provided.
Q2. Who receives the amount due?
A. The amount due is payable to the deceased partner's legal representative/estate according to the applicable arrangement.

7. Dissolution of Partnership Firm

Dissolution closes the firm's business and settles assets and liabilities. Realisation Account records disposal of assets and settlement of liabilities.

Q1. What is Realisation Account?
A. It is a nominal account prepared to record realisation of assets, settlement of liabilities and resulting profit or loss.
Q2. What is the order of settlement?
A. External liabilities are settled first, followed by partner loans and partners' capitals, subject to applicable rules.
Q3. How is realisation profit/loss distributed?
A. Among partners in their profit-sharing ratio.

Numerical practice: Prepare Realisation, Partners' Capital and Cash/Bank Accounts for a firm with given assets, liabilities, realisation expenses and settlement details.

8. Share Capital

Share capital is the capital raised through issue of shares. Key terms: authorised, issued, subscribed, called-up and paid-up capital.

Issue Process

Application → Allotment → Calls. Oversubscription may involve rejection, refund or pro-rata allotment.

Q1. What is authorised capital?
A. The maximum share capital a company is authorised to issue under its constitutional documents.
Q2. What is oversubscription?
A. When applications received exceed the number of shares offered for subscription.
Q3. What is forfeiture?
A. Cancellation of shares due to non-payment of amounts due according to the terms of issue and applicable rules.
Q4. What is reissue?
A. Reissue is the subsequent issue of forfeited shares subject to applicable rules.

9. Debentures

Debentures represent borrowed funds and debenture holders are creditors.

Interest = Nominal Value × Rate × Time ÷ 100
Q1. Who is a debenture holder?
A. A debenture holder is a creditor of the company.
Q2. What is redemption?
A. Redemption is repayment of debenture liability according to the terms of issue.
Q3. Debentures of ₹2,00,000 carry 10% annual interest. Find one year's interest.
A. ₹2,00,000 × 10% = ₹20,000.

10. Financial Statements of a Company

Financial statements communicate the financial position and performance of a company.

Main Statements

  • Balance Sheet
  • Statement of Profit and Loss
  • Notes to Accounts
Q1. What is a Balance Sheet?
A. It presents assets, equity and liabilities at a particular date according to the prescribed format.
Q2. What is Statement of Profit and Loss?
A. It summarises income and expenses for an accounting period to determine profit or loss.
Q3. Why are Notes to Accounts important?
A. They provide supporting details and disclosures for items presented in the financial statements.

11. Analysis of Financial Statements

Analysis evaluates profitability, liquidity, solvency and efficiency.

Absolute Change = Current Year − Previous Year
Percentage Change = Absolute Change ÷ Previous Year × 100
Q1. What is comparative analysis?
A. It compares financial statement figures across periods and highlights absolute and percentage changes.
Q2. What is a common-size statement?
A. A statement where each item is expressed as a percentage of a common base.
Q3. Previous sales ₹4,00,000 and current sales ₹5,00,000. Find percentage change.
A. Change = ₹1,00,000; percentage change = 1,00,000/4,00,000 × 100 = 25%.

12. Accounting Ratios

Important Formula Sheet

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio = Quick Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Debt-Equity Ratio = Long-term Debt ÷ Shareholders' Funds
Gross Profit Ratio = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100
Net Profit Ratio = Net Profit ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100
Operating Ratio = Operating Cost ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100
Inventory Turnover = Cost of Revenue from Operations ÷ Average Inventory
Q1. Current Assets ₹3,00,000 and Current Liabilities ₹1,50,000. Find current ratio.
A. ₹3,00,000 ÷ ₹1,50,000 = 2:1.
Q2. Gross profit ₹80,000 and revenue ₹4,00,000. Find GP ratio.
A. ₹80,000/₹4,00,000 × 100 = 20%.
Q3. Why are ratios useful?
A. They simplify relationships between accounting figures and help assess liquidity, solvency, profitability and efficiency.

13. Cash Flow Statement

Cash Flow Statement explains cash and cash-equivalent inflows and outflows during an accounting period.

Three Activities

  • Operating: main revenue-generating activities.
  • Investing: purchase/sale of long-term assets and investments.
  • Financing: changes in equity and borrowings, subject to applicable classification rules.
Net Change in Cash = Operating Cash Flow + Investing Cash Flow + Financing Cash Flow
Q1. What is operating cash flow?
A. Cash generated or used by the main revenue-generating activities of the business.
Q2. What is investing cash flow?
A. Cash arising from purchase and sale of long-term assets and investments.
Q3. What is financing cash flow?
A. Cash flows resulting in changes in equity and borrowing structure, subject to the applicable rules.
