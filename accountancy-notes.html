1. Partnership Fundamentals

Partnership is the relationship between persons who agree to share profits of a business carried on by all or any one of them acting for all. The key ideas are agreement, business, profit sharing, mutual agency and competent partners.

Partnership Deed

A written agreement normally states capital contribution, profit-sharing ratio, drawings, interest on capital/drawings, partner salary or commission, admission/retirement terms and dispute arrangements.

When the deed is silent

  • Profits and losses are shared equally.
  • No interest on capital is allowed.
  • No interest on drawings is charged.
  • A partner is not entitled to salary/commission merely for working.
  • Interest on a partner's loan is allowed according to applicable partnership rules.

Capital Accounts

Under the fixed-capital method, capital remains unchanged except for permanent changes and a separate Current Account records drawings, interest, salary and share of profit. Under fluctuating capital, all such adjustments are made directly in Capital Accounts.

Profit and Loss Appropriation

It distributes the firm's profit after considering interest on capital, partner salary/commission and interest on drawings.

Partner's Share = Distributable Profit × Partner's Profit-Sharing Fraction

Exam tip: Read the deed first, then identify the adjustment required and pass entries in the correct order.

2. Goodwill

Goodwill is the value of business reputation and other advantages that enable a firm to earn higher profits than normal.

Average Profit Method

Average Profit = Total Adjusted Profits ÷ Number of Years
Goodwill = Average Profit × Years' Purchase

Super Profit Method

Normal Profit = Capital Employed × Normal Rate ÷ 100
Super Profit = Average Profit − Normal Profit
Goodwill = Super Profit × Years' Purchase

Capitalisation Methods

Capitalised Value of Average Profit = Average Profit × 100 ÷ Normal Rate
Goodwill = Capitalised Value − Actual Capital Employed
Capitalised Value of Super Profit = Super Profit × 100 ÷ Normal Rate

Adjust profits for abnormal gains/losses and future expected changes before calculating average profit. Goodwill is commonly adjusted on admission, retirement, death and change in ratio.

Exam tip: Always distinguish old ratio, new ratio, sacrificing ratio and gaining ratio before goodwill adjustment.

3. Change in Profit-Sharing Ratio

Existing partners may change their profit-sharing ratio by agreement. The main adjustments are goodwill, reserves/accumulated profits and revaluation.

Sacrificing Ratio = Old Share − New Share
Gaining Ratio = New Share − Old Share
  • Partners who gain compensate partners who sacrifice for goodwill.
  • Existing reserves and accumulated profits/losses are distributed in the old ratio unless the question specifies otherwise.
  • Assets and liabilities may be revalued and the resulting profit/loss is shared in the old ratio.

Exam tip: Use fractions, not only ratios, when calculating each partner's change in share.

4. Admission of a Partner

A new partner is admitted with the consent of existing partners. The incoming partner usually brings capital and goodwill and receives a share of future profits.

Standard sequence

  1. Calculate the new profit-sharing ratio.
  2. Calculate sacrificing ratio of old partners.
  3. Adjust goodwill.
  4. Revalue assets and liabilities through Revaluation Account.
  5. Distribute reserves and accumulated profits/losses.
  6. Bring in new partner's capital.
  7. Prepare revised Balance Sheet.

Goodwill brought in cash

If the incoming partner brings premium for goodwill, it is credited to sacrificing partners in their sacrificing ratio, subject to the method given in the question.

Revaluation

Increase in asset or decrease in liability is a gain; decrease in asset or increase in liability is a loss. The resulting profit/loss is transferred to old partners in their old ratio.

5. Retirement of a Partner

On retirement, the remaining partners continue the business. The retiring partner is entitled to the amount due under the agreement.

Gaining Ratio = New Share − Old Share
  • Calculate new ratio of continuing partners.
  • Calculate gaining ratio.
  • Adjust goodwill in favour of the retiring partner.
  • Adjust revaluation profit/loss.
  • Transfer reserves and accumulated profits/losses.
  • Calculate amount payable and settlement method.

If the amount is not paid immediately, it may be transferred to the retiring partner's loan account as required by the question.

6. Death of a Partner

The accounting treatment is similar to retirement, but the deceased partner's estate is entitled to the amount due up to the date of death.

Amount due may include

  • Capital balance
  • Share of goodwill
  • Share of reserves and accumulated profits
  • Share of revaluation profit
  • Share of profit up to the date of death
  • Less drawings, losses and other applicable deductions

Profit up to date of death

It can be estimated using time basis or sales/turnover basis when the question provides the necessary information.

7. Dissolution of Partnership Firm

Dissolution of the firm means business is closed and assets/liabilities are settled. The Realisation Account is used to record disposal of assets and settlement of liabilities.

Order of settlement

  1. Realisation of assets and payment of external liabilities.
  2. Partner's loan is settled after external liabilities, according to applicable rules.
  3. Partners' capitals are settled.
  4. Remaining cash is distributed according to entitlement.

Realisation profit/loss is transferred to partners' Capital Accounts in the profit-sharing ratio.

Realisation Result = Net effect of assets realised and liabilities settled plus realisation expenses

Exam tip: Do not transfer cash/bank to Realisation Account unless the question specifically requires the applicable treatment.

8. Share Capital

Share capital is the amount raised by a company through issue of shares. Important terms include authorised, issued, subscribed, called-up and paid-up capital.

Issue of shares

  • Application: money received with applications.
  • Allotment: shares are allotted and allotment money becomes due.
  • Calls: further instalments demanded according to issue terms.
  • Oversubscription may require rejection, refund or pro-rata allotment.

Forfeiture and Reissue

Forfeiture cancels shares when a shareholder fails to pay due amounts according to the company's terms. Reissue is a fresh issue of forfeited shares subject to applicable company law and accounting rules. Any remaining balance in Share Forfeiture related to reissued shares is transferred as required.

9. Debentures

Debentures represent borrowed funds; debenture holders are creditors. They generally receive interest according to the terms of issue.

  • Issue at par, premium or discount where permitted.
  • Issue for consideration other than cash.
  • Interest on debentures is a charge against profit.
  • Redemption means repayment of debenture liability according to terms.
  • Debentures may be issued with security and other conditions as stated in the question.
Interest = Nominal Value of Debentures × Rate × Time ÷ 100

10. Financial Statements of a Company

Financial statements communicate the financial position and performance of a company.

Balance Sheet

It presents assets, equity and liabilities at a particular date under the prescribed format.

Statement of Profit and Loss

It summarises income and expenses for the accounting period and arrives at profit/loss.

Notes to Accounts

Notes provide supporting details, accounting policies and disclosures related to financial statement items.

Exam tip: Learn the prescribed headings and classify items carefully before placing them in the format.

11. Analysis of Financial Statements

Financial statement analysis evaluates profitability, liquidity, solvency and efficiency.

Comparative Statements

Absolute Change = Current Year − Previous Year
Percentage Change = Absolute Change ÷ Previous Year × 100

Common-size Statements

Each item is shown as a percentage of a common base, helping comparison across years or firms.

  • Liquidity: ability to meet short-term obligations.
  • Solvency: ability to meet long-term obligations.
  • Profitability: ability to earn adequate returns.
  • Efficiency: effectiveness of resource utilisation.

12. Accounting Ratios

Liquidity Ratios

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio = Quick Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Solvency Ratios

Debt-Equity Ratio = Long-term Debt ÷ Shareholders' Funds
Proprietary Ratio = Shareholders' Funds ÷ Total Assets

Activity Ratios

Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Revenue from Operations ÷ Average Inventory
Trade Receivables Turnover = Net Credit Revenue from Operations ÷ Average Trade Receivables
Working Capital Turnover = Revenue from Operations ÷ Working Capital

Profitability Ratios

Gross Profit Ratio = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100
Net Profit Ratio = Net Profit ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100
Operating Ratio = Operating Cost ÷ Revenue from Operations × 100

Exam tip: Write the formula first, substitute figures second, calculate third, and interpret the result when asked.

13. Cash Flow Statement

A Cash Flow Statement explains inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents during an accounting period.

Operating Activities

Main revenue-generating activities. Under the indirect method, start with profit and adjust for non-cash/non-operating items and changes in working capital.

Investing Activities

Purchase and sale of long-term assets and investments. Purchase generally creates an outflow; sale generally creates an inflow.

Financing Activities

Transactions changing equity and borrowings, such as issue/redemption of shares or debentures and borrowings/repayments, subject to the applicable classification rules.

Net Change in Cash = Operating Cash Flow + Investing Cash Flow + Financing Cash Flow

Exam tip: Practise classification of each transaction before attempting a full cash-flow question.
